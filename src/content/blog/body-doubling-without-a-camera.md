Every well-known body doubling tool — Focusmate, Flow Club, Caveday — solves
the same problem the same way: put another person's live video feed next to
your work, and their presence keeps you on task. For a lot of people that
works. For some people, the requirement to be on camera, presentable,
watched, is itself the thing standing between them and starting.

## What body doubling is actually doing

Body doubling works, where it works, through a genuinely old and
well-established idea: social facilitation. The finding — first documented
by Norman Triplett in one of psychology's earliest experiments, back in
1898 — is that people often perform simple or well-practiced tasks better
in the mere presence of others, even without any interaction happening at
all. No coaching, no accountability check-in required. Presence alone does
some of the work.

It's worth being honest about the evidence here too: no one has run a
proper controlled trial specifically on body doubling for ADHD. What's well
established is the older social facilitation research it draws on; the leap
to "and this specifically helps ADHD focus" is a reasonable, widely reported
inference from lived experience, not a settled clinical finding.

## Why the camera is the actual sticking point for some people

If presence alone is doing the work, a live video feed is one *implementation*
of presence, not the only possible one — and it's an implementation that
adds its own cost. Being watched changes behavior beyond just "on task,"
and for someone with social anxiety, RSD, or simply a home that isn't
camera-ready at 9am, the setup cost of finding a body-doubling session can
outweigh the benefit before the work has even started.

| Requirement | Focusmate / Flow Club style | Ambient / no-camera style |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A scheduled time slot | Yes | No |
| Being visibly on camera | Yes | No |
| A real human's presence | Yes, live | No — a companion mechanic instead |
| Setup friction before starting | Booking, camera check, small talk | None |

## An ambient version of the same mechanism

A virtual companion doesn't replicate a human's presence — it isn't trying
to. What it offers is a lighter-weight version of the same underlying
signal: something else is here, paying attention to whether you're working,
with none of the scheduling or camera requirement attached. [Body doubling
with a virtual pet](/body-doubling-with-a-virtual-pet/) is built around
exactly this trade — less social proof than a real human, but available in
zero seconds, with zero camera, whenever the alternative would otherwise be
working entirely alone.

Neither version is strictly better. If live accountability with a real
person genuinely works for you, that's a stronger form of the same effect
than any app can offer. But if the camera has been the reason you never
actually book the session, an ambient companion removes the one part of the
setup that was stopping you before the work began.
