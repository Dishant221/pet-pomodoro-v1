A desk companion helps deep work by doing almost nothing. That is not a
limitation to apologise for; it is the entire job description. Deep work
advice is otherwise a long list of subtractions — block the sites, mute the
phone, empty the room — and the subtractions are right, but an emptied room
turns out to be a surprisingly hard place to sit for three hours.

The companion is the one addition worth making, and choosing it well comes
down to a single question: what does it do while you are working? The right
answer is: sleep.

## Deep work advice is a list of things to remove

The standard prescription is correct as far as it goes. Distraction is the
enemy of depth, so you strip the environment: notifications off, tabs
closed, door shut, desk cleared. Every item on the list is a subtraction,
and each one genuinely helps.

But follow the list to its end and you arrive somewhere odd — a silent,
featureless room containing you, a task, and nothing else. Some people
thrive there. A lot of people find, without ever quite saying it, that the
sterile room is why their deep work sessions do not happen. The silence has
a pressure to it. There is nothing in the room but the difficulty of the
task, and when the task gets hard there is nowhere for the eye to rest that
isn't the task or the exit.

Subtraction solves distraction. It does nothing for desolation, and
desolation sends you to your phone just as reliably.

## Presence as ballast

What the empty room lacks is ballast — something alive-ish in the space
whose presence asks nothing of you. People who work well in libraries and
quiet cafés know the effect: nobody there helps you, nobody speaks, and yet
the work sits steadier than it does at home in a silent flat. Other beings,
peacefully occupied, hold a room still.

Part of this is the witness effect that makes [body
doubling](/blog/body-doubling-with-a-virtual-pet/) work — being observably
at your desk makes leaving it feel like a small event rather than nothing.
Part is simpler: company that expects nothing is
[settling in a way silence is not](/blog/presence-without-conversation/).
A sleeping cat on the end of the bed does not help you sleep, exactly. The
bed is calmer with it there.

A desk companion imports that stillness to the desk. It is not a tool, in
the sense of doing anything. It is furniture with a pulse.

## During focus, the companion must be boring

Here is the design question that separates a companion from a distraction,
and it is worth being blunt about: anything animated, cute and *active* on
your screen while you work is an enemy of the work. An animal doing
delightful things in the corner of your eye is precisely the thing a deep
work setup exists to remove. Add that, and you have subtracted the phone
only to install a replacement.

So the companion must be at its least interesting exactly when your
attention is most valuable. In PetPomo, starting a focus session sends the
cat to sleep — it curls up, stops asking for anything, and becomes a small,
still shape at the edge of the screen. All its charm is queued for the
break, when it wakes wanting food and fuss and attention is the thing you
are supposed to be spending. The ordering is the whole design:
[a reason to start and a reason not to abandon](/blog/why-a-virtual-pet-helps-you-focus/),
and stillness in between.

A companion that respects attention is one whose maker had to choose
between being adorable and being useful, and chose useful. It is a rarer
choice than it should be.

## The quiet spectrum, and where pixels sit on it

None of this requires software. The desk companion spectrum runs from a
co-working friend, through a dog asleep by the chair, down to a plant you
have opinions about — and every point on it works by the same mechanism of
undemanding presence. If a real animal shares your home, it is probably
already doing this job better than anything on a screen.

The case for the pixel version is practical, not superior. It is there at
3 p.m. in an office where a dog is not. It never wants a walk during your
best hour — its needs are considerately scheduled inside your breaks. It
costs nothing to keep and asks no social performance of you. And it is
tied to the work itself: the cat's day is structured by your sessions,
which no spaniel will ever agree to.

The honest framing is that a virtual companion is the lightest, most
portable point on a spectrum of real things. Lighter also means thinner —
it is presence, not friendship — and anyone who tells you otherwise is
selling something. (What PetPomo is and isn't trying to be is laid out
[on the about page](/about/), and "friendship" is not on the list.)

## Choosing one, if you do

If you try a desk companion for deep work, judge it on three behaviours.
While you focus, it should be still and silent — no idle animations
begging for a glance. When you finish, it should notice; a finished
session deserves a wag, a stretch, a small celebration, because that is
the moment motivation is actually built. And when you have been away, it
should be pleased rather than reproachful — a companion that greets your
return with guilt is a scoreboard wearing fur.

Then give it a fair test: a week of real sessions, deep work of the kind
you actually struggle with. Some people will find the corner of the
screen is simply occupied now, and that a room with something sleeping in
it is easier to stay in. Others will feel nothing and should go back to
the beautifully empty room with a clear conscience.

Both results are fine. The empty room was never wrong — it was just,
for some of us, missing a cat.
