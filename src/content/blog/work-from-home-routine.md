The most workable work-from-home routine is built around when you stop, not
how many hours you sit. Hours are how offices measure presence; at home nobody
is measuring, and the day holds together far better when its skeleton is a set
of deliberate breaks with work stretched between them.

That sounds backwards until you notice what actually disappeared when you
stopped commuting. It was not the desk, and it was not the colleagues exactly.
It was the edges.

## The office gave your day a shape, and you never noticed

A commute is a terrible way to spend an hour, but it does one thing perfectly:
it separates work from not-work with a physical journey. You could not
accidentally still be at your desk at nine in the evening, because your desk
was somewhere else.

Inside the office, more edges were being drawn for you all day. The kettle
queue was a break you did not schedule. Lunch happened because other people
stood up. The day ended because the room emptied and staying felt strange.
None of this was designed — it was ambient structure, and it left when you
did.

The [missing company](/blog/working-from-home-isolation/) gets written about a
great deal. The missing edges get less attention, and they may matter just as
much, because a day without edges does not become more productive. It becomes
a single grey smear in which you are somehow always working and never quite
working.

## Why breaks make a better skeleton than hours

The obvious fix is to impose office hours on yourself — nine to five-thirty,
at the desk. Many people try it, and it tends to dissolve within a week,
because hours are passive. Nothing happens at the boundary of an hour. There
is no event, so there is nothing to keep.

A break is an event. It has a beginning, a content and an end, and events are
what days are actually made of. If you plan the stops — mid-morning,
lunch, mid-afternoon, finish — the work arranges itself into the gaps
without much further management. You stop asking "have I done enough hours?"
(unanswerable, and the answer always feels like no) and start asking "did I
work the stretches between the breaks?" — which has a plain yes or no.

This is the pomodoro idea scaled up to a whole day: the rest is not the
opposite of the structure, the rest *is* the structure.

## A break and a drift are not the same thing

Here is where home working quietly goes wrong. What most people call a break
is actually a drift: the hand reaches for the phone, the scroll begins, and
twenty minutes later you surface feeling worse than when you stopped. A drift
has no edges — that is precisely what makes it restful-looking and
restless-feeling — and it does not end so much as get interrupted by guilt.

A real break has content. Stand up. Fill the kettle. Look out of a window at
something further away than a screen. The content matters because it gives
the break a natural end: the tea is made, the stretch is done, you sit back
down. Skipping breaks entirely is the other failure mode, and it costs more
than it saves — the case against it is [worth reading on its
own](/blog/skipping-breaks-adhd/), because a skipped break is really a loan
taken out against the next work stretch.

This is the reason PetPomo puts the pet's whole social life inside the break.
The cat sleeps while you focus and wants feeding, stroking or a game when the
bell rings — which means the break has a small job in it, and a job with an
end is much harder to drift through than an open scroll.

## What this looks like across an actual day

Start with a ritual rather than a resolution. The same mug, the same seat,
the first timer started — a short fixed sequence that tells your head the day
has begun, the way the commute used to. [Small rituals carry more of solitary
work than they appear to](/blog/small-rituals-solitary-work/), and the start
of the day is where they earn the most.

Then work in stretches you can actually finish, with a short break at the end
of each and a proper long one at lunch — away from the desk, ideally away
from the room. If you use [a timer with a pet attached](/), the rhythm mostly
runs itself: focus until the bell, spend the break on the cat and the kettle,
sit back down. The pet does not know it is enforcing a routine. That is
rather the point.

Keep the afternoon honest by planning fewer stretches than you think fit. A
home day holds less deep work than the empty calendar suggests, and a plan
with slack in it survives contact with reality; a packed one collapses by
three o'clock and takes your mood with it.

## End the day on purpose

The nastiest failure of home working is not laziness. It is the day that
never ends — the laptop still open at nine, not because you are working but
because you never decided to stop, and half-working all evening is a
remarkably efficient way to get neither work nor rest.

So decide the last bell in advance. When it rings, write two lines about
where tomorrow starts, close the laptop, and physically leave the desk, even
if you only walk to the kitchen. The note is what lets you stop — an open
task with a known next step will wait quietly overnight; an open task without
one nags all evening.

PetPomo's scenes shift with the real time of day, so by the last session the
room on screen has gone to dusk. It is a small thing, but a small thing that
says *the day is ending* is exactly what the home office lacks.

None of this needs to be got right first time. Build the edges, defend the
breaks, end on purpose, and let the hours look after themselves — they were
never the point.
