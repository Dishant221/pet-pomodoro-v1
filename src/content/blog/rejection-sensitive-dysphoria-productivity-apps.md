A lot of habit apps are built on a quiet threat: keep your streak, or feel
bad about losing it. For most people that's just mildly annoying. For
someone whose ADHD comes with rejection-sensitive dysphoria, a broken streak
isn't mildly annoying — it can read as proof of something, and the app
designed to help just delivered the evidence.

## What RSD actually is

Rejection sensitive dysphoria — a term the psychiatrist William Dodson
popularized in the 1990s to describe extreme emotional pain from perceived
criticism or failure — isn't a formal diagnosis in the DSM-5, but the
experience it names is increasingly taken seriously in research. A recent
qualitative study of adults with ADHD found a recurring theme: the
*anticipation* of rejection or failure was often described as more painful
than the thing itself actually happening, consistent with a sensitization
pattern where early experiences of criticism amplify later emotional
reactions. A separate qualitative study identified withdrawal, masking, and
physical bodily sensations as common responses to perceived rejection in
ADHD adults.

## Why streak-shame is the wrong design for this

A habit app that shows "you broke your 47-day streak" in red isn't neutral
information — for someone prone to RSD, it can land as a verdict. And the
predictable response to a verdict like that isn't "I'll try again tomorrow."
It's often avoidance: don't open the app at all, so it can't deliver that
verdict again. The tool built to create consistency ends up actively
punishing the exact person who most needed a forgiving one.

This connects to a broader pattern — abandonment isn't always about losing
interest. Sometimes the app itself becomes something to avoid, which is a
large part of why [streaks specifically are a stressful design
choice](/streaks-are-stressful/) rather than a neutral one.

## What a kinder design actually looks like

PetPomo's mood system is built around this specifically: the cat gets a
little sad if you've been away, and delighted when you come back — but
nothing resets to zero, nothing is stamped "failed," and there's no
red streak counter anywhere to break. A missed day changes the pet's mood,
which recovers the moment you show up again. That's a meaningfully
different message than a counter that publicly restarts at zero.

| What a streak-shame design does | What a mood-based design does instead |
| --- | --- |
| Marks a miss as a reset to zero | Reflects a miss as a temporary dip, not an erasure |
| Presents the miss publicly (a number visibly drops) | Presents it privately, as a mood only you're checking in on |
| Implicitly frames consistency as all-or-nothing | Recovers gradually — nothing to "break" |

## If this is you

Noticing that a specific app's design pattern makes you avoid the app,
rather than use it more, isn't a character flaw — it's useful information
about the tool, not about you. Forgiving-by-design tools tend to hold up
better over months than anything that quietly threatens you into
consistency.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment, and RSD is a real and sometimes
significant experience — nothing here is medical advice, and a therapist
familiar with ADHD is the right person to talk to about it in depth.
