With ADHD, starting a task is often harder than doing it. The work itself
might be fine — easy, even enjoyable, once you are in it. The cost lives
entirely in the beginning: that stretch between fully intending to start and
actually starting, which can swallow an hour, an afternoon, or the whole
day. This has a name, task initiation, and it responds far better to
shrinking the commitment and ritualising the start than it ever does to
willpower.

## The wall is real, and it is not laziness

The experience is strange to describe to anyone who hasn't had it. You are
sitting at the desk. You know what the task is. You want it done — sometimes
desperately. And you do not start. Not because you decided to do something
else; the deciding machinery simply doesn't fire. You can watch yourself not
starting the way you'd watch weather.

From the outside this looks exactly like laziness, which is why so many
people with ADHD carry a lifetime's stock of shame about it. But laziness is
not wanting the thing done. This is wanting it done and being unable to
begin — a wall between intention and action, and the wall's height has
almost nothing to do with how much the task matters. Filing an expense claim
and starting a dream project can be equally impossible on the same morning.

What the wall responds to is not importance, and not guilt. It responds to
the *size of the commitment* on the other side.

## The size of the yes sets the height of the wall

When you go to start "the report", you are not really saying yes to typing a
sentence. You are saying yes to the entire report — the unclear parts, the
boring middle, the hours it might take, the version of the evening where it
swallows everything. That is what the beginning weighs. No wonder the
machinery stalls.

So shrink what you are saying yes to, on two axes at once.

**One session, not the task.** Commit to a fixed stretch of minutes, after
which you are genuinely allowed to stop. This is the quiet reason
[the Pomodoro Technique suits many people with ADHD](/blog/pomodoro-technique-adhd/):
it replaces "do the report" with "sit with the report until a bell rings",
and the second yes is radically cheaper. The bell is load-bearing — the
permission to stop is what makes the start affordable.

**One task, not a list.** A session pointed at three things is really
pointed at none, and choosing between them is itself a start-cost you'll pay
at minute one. Name a single task before you begin —
[one task per session](/blog/one-task-per-session/) is the rule that keeps
the interval honest.

Get both right and the thing you must say yes to is no longer "the report".
It is "fifteen minutes on the intro". Walls have a much harder time forming
in front of that.

## A ritual moves the start out of your head

Here is the other half. Even a small yes still needs the deciding machinery
to fire — unless you take deciding out of it entirely.

That is what a start ritual is for. A ritual is a short, fixed sequence of
physical actions that ends with the work having begun: same mug, same seat,
write the task on a scrap of paper, press the button. Done identically every
time, the sequence stops being a series of decisions and becomes one motion.
You are no longer initiating a task — a thing you are bad at — you are
running a routine, a thing bodies do rather well. The start stops being a
leap and becomes a ramp.

The button at the end of the ritual matters more than it looks. In
[PetPomo](/) pressing start does something visible: the cat curls up and
goes to sleep. That small ceremony marks the boundary — a state change in
the world, outside your head, that says *it has begun*. Crossing a line
someone else drew is easier than drawing the line yourself, even when the
someone else is a cartoon cat. The session is now a thing that exists, with
you inside it.

Rituals feel silly to set up precisely because they are so mechanical. That
is the point. The wall is not mechanical — it is fog — and mechanism is what
cuts through fog.

## Make the first minute stupid

One refinement pays for itself: decide, before the session, what the first
sixty seconds will be — and make it embarrassingly small. Not "start the
report": *open the document and read the last paragraph aloud*. Not "do the
expenses": *find the receipt photo on your phone*.

The first minute is the wall's whole territory. Once you are past it, the
task's own momentum usually takes over — the ADHD problem was never
sustaining engagement with something you've entered, it was entering. A
stupid first minute is a door handle at hand height.

## Let the finish pay you immediately

Last piece: starts get cheaper when finishes pay. Not next month, when the
project ships — *at the bell*. A distant reward is weightless against a
present wall; a small immediate one actually tips the scales, which is why
the coin at the end of a PetPomo session lands the way a quarterly bonus
never does. Why small-and-now beats large-and-later — and where that kind
of design stays honest — is worth
[its own article](/blog/small-rewards-adhd-motivation/), but the practical
rule is simple: arrange for something pleasant to happen at the moment a
session ends, every time. The next start is bought with the last finish.

None of this makes starting effortless. It makes starting *cheap enough to
afford on an ordinary day*, which is the realistic target. The wall doesn't
come down; you build a lower gate.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
focus problems are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the right
person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.
