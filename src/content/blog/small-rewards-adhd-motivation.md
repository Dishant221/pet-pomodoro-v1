Motivation with ADHD works best when the reward arrives now, not at the end of
term. Distant payoffs — the grade, the promotion, the finished thesis — are
real, but by the time they have travelled from months away to the present
moment they have lost nearly all their pull, while a small reward that lands
the second the bell rings arrives at full weight. This is not a character
flaw to be trained out. It is a fact about how delay feels, and the useful
move is to design around it rather than argue with it.

## Distant rewards shrink; near ones don't

Everyone discounts the future a little. Ask anyone whether they would rather
have something good today or something slightly better in six months and watch
them hesitate. With ADHD the discounting is steeper — a reward three weeks
away is not a smaller version of itself, it is functionally invisible. You can
know the exam matters, believe it matters, and still find the knowledge
weightless at four o'clock on a Tuesday when the revision is in front of you.

The standard advice — *keep your eyes on the goal* — asks you to run the whole
motivational supply line from a warehouse months away. When the line is that
long, almost nothing arrives. The alternative is to build a depot next to the
work: something small, certain, and immediate that fires every time a block of
work actually happens.

This is also why the wall at the start of a task is so high. If the payoff is
distant and the cost is right now, the ledger for *beginning* looks terrible —
there is a whole piece on that wall in
[task initiation with ADHD](/blog/adhd-task-initiation/). A near reward does
not demolish the wall, but it puts something visible on the other side of it.

## The reward must follow the finish, not the effort

Here is the detail that most reward systems fumble: what the reward is
attached to.

Attach it to *time spent* and you reward sitting there. Attach it to *opening
the app* and you reward opening the app — plenty of tools have discovered
their users are excellent at logging in. The honest anchor is the finish: the
session either ended with the bell or it did not, and the reward follows only
the bell.

This sounds strict and is actually kind. It makes the reward mean something,
which is the whole reason it can pull you through minute nineteen. In
PetPomo, the coin drops when the session completes, and the cat wakes up
pleased — an abandoned session earns nothing, quietly, without a lecture. The
gap between *nothing* and *something* does more work than any punishment
could, because it points forward: the next session is always worth full
price.

Small matters here too. A reward that is too large stops being a marker and
starts being the point, and then the work becomes the annoying thing standing
between you and the reward. A coin, a tick, a cat's stretch — tokens, really.
Their job is not to pay you. Their job is to make the finish *land*, to give
the moment a shape you can feel, so that finishing stops being an abstraction
and starts being an event.

## The reward is a receipt, and receipts accumulate

One coin is trivial. A pattern of them is not. The quiet second function of
small immediate rewards is that they leave a trail — evidence, on a bad day,
that you are a person who finishes sessions, because there they are, counted.

This is worth more with ADHD than it might sound, because memory for one's own
effort is patchy and the felt sense of "I never get anything done" is often
flatly contradicted by the record. A [stats page](/stats/) that shows the
sessions you actually completed is the same receipt at a longer timescale —
not a scoreboard to beat, just a mirror that remembers better than you do.

## Where gamification tips into manipulation

There is a line here and it is worth drawing carefully, because the same
machinery that makes finishing feel good can be pointed at you instead of at
the work.

The tell is what the system does with your absence. An honest reward adds
something when you finish. A manipulative one *removes* something when you
don't — the streak that shatters, the plant that dies, the creature that
suffers visibly until you come back. Both use the same loop; only one of them
is on your side. Loss-flavoured mechanics work, in the narrow sense that fear
works, but they convert a focus tool into a small anxiety machine, and people
quite sensibly walk away from those — it is one of the reasons
[productivity apps get abandoned](/blog/why-productivity-apps-get-abandoned/)
so quickly.

The test to apply to any reward system, including PetPomo's: *what happens if
I disappear for two weeks?* If the answer is "you return to wreckage", the
rewards were a leash. If the answer is "you return to a slightly sad cat who
is delighted to see you and nothing has been lost", the rewards were what they
claimed to be — a way of making good moments legible, not a way of making
leaving expensive.

## Rewards start the engine; they don't have to drive

A fair worry: doesn't this make you dependent on trinkets? Won't you become
someone who can only work for coins?

Experience mostly runs the other way. The reward's job is heaviest at the
start — first minute, first session, first week — when the work has no
momentum of its own. Once a rhythm exists, the work starts generating its own
returns: the session finished yesterday makes today's easier to start, and
finished work has a satisfaction that no token matches. The coin is a
jump-lead, not the engine. There is a longer argument about why a creature
makes a better jump-lead than a number in
[why a virtual pet helps you focus](/blog/why-a-virtual-pet-helps-you-focus/).

So: make the reward small, make it immediate, attach it to the finish, and
never let it punish you. That combination is unglamorous and it holds up.
