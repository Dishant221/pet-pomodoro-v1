The real cost of a context switch is not the moment you glance at the other
thing — it is the reload afterwards, when you have to rebuild everything you
were holding in your head before the glance. The glance takes a second. The
rebuilding is what quietly eats the afternoon.

This is why context switching feels cheap while it is happening and expensive
only in retrospect, at five o'clock, when the day is somehow gone and the
hard task is somehow still open.

## You pay for the reload, not the glance

Hold a piece of real work in your head — a document half-argued, a bug
half-cornered, a plan half-formed — and you are holding more than the words
on the screen. You are holding what you were about to do next, which
approaches you have already ruled out, where the tricky part is, and a
half-formed sentence that exists nowhere except in your head.

None of that is written down, because writing it all down would take longer
than the work. It lives in working memory, and working memory does not pause
when you switch away. It drains. Answer one quick message and you return to
find the structure partly collapsed: the next step is foggy, the ruled-out
paths look open again, the held sentence has gone. So you re-read, re-derive,
re-climb — and the re-climbing is the cost. The message took a moment; being
*back* took much longer, and nothing on any clock recorded the difference.

## The cost is invisible because switching feels like work

Nobody experiences a context switch as slacking. Answering the message *was*
work. Checking the ticket *was* work. That is precisely what makes the habit
so durable: every individual switch is defensible, and the price is paid
later, diffusely, as a general sense of having been busy all day to no
effect.

A day of constant switching produces a distinctive signature — lots of things
touched, nothing finished, and a tiredness out of proportion to anything
accomplished. The tiredness is real. It is the cost of a dozen reloads,
experienced as one long afternoon. This is the fog that makes [office
pomodoro use](/blog/pomodoro-at-work/) feel so different from working at
whim: not that the timer makes you faster, but that it stops you paying the
reload tax a dozen times before lunch.

## A bounded session is a contract against switching

The pomodoro's countdown looks like the whole mechanism, but the deeper one
is the boundary itself. When you start a session on [one named
task](/blog/one-task-per-session/), you have signed a small contract: for
this bounded stretch, everything else waits. Not forever — that would be
unreasonable, and unreasonable promises get broken. Just until the bell.

The contract works because it converts an open question into a closed one.
Without it, every arriving thought demands a fresh decision — deal with this
now or later? — and each decision is itself a small switch. With it, the
answer is pre-made: later, and *later* has a concrete time attached. The
thought goes on the scrap of paper beside the keyboard and loses most of its
power on the way down, because a written-down thing stops needing to be
remembered, and being remembered was most of its noise.

In PetPomo the contract has a witness, which helps more than it should. The
cat lies down when the session starts; abandoning the session means
abandoning it mid-nap. Nothing terrible happens — the cat gets sad, nothing
is lost — but the switch is no longer free and frictionless, and a switch
with even a feather of friction on it gives you the half-second in which to
choose not to.

## Give the shallow work a session of its own

The messages, the small approvals, the five-minute errands — none of it is
illegitimate work, and pretending you can ignore it forever is how the
contract gets broken. The fix is not to resist shallow work but to *batch*
it: give it its own session, with its own start and its own bell, and do all
of it at once.

Batching wins twice. Each individual reply escapes its own reload cost,
because replying to twelve messages in a row keeps you in one shallow
context instead of yanking you through twelve deep-to-shallow round trips.
And the deep work gets what it actually needs, which is not more hours but
*unbroken* ones — a distinction that matters most when the hours are scarce,
as anyone [hunting focus between meetings](/blog/focus-blocks-between-meetings/)
already knows. One clean session in a fragmented day outweighs a whole
morning of interleaved everything.

## The notification question

Which leaves notifications, the industrial machinery of the context switch.
The uncomfortable truth is that a notification does its damage even when you
do not act on it. The buzz you heroically ignore still lands, because
ignoring it takes a decision, and the decision is itself the switch — small,
but you have already paid for the glance-sized part.

So during a focus session the goal is not resisting notifications but not
receiving them: phone face down and silent, the noisy tabs closed, the chat
app quit rather than minimised. This feels drastic until you remember the
contract's other clause — it ends. The bell rings, the messages get their
turn, and anything genuinely urgent has a phone number. A [session
timer](/) is, in the end, just a machine for making "not now" both honest
and temporary.

Context switching will never cost nothing; work is interruptible and life
is not tidy. But the tax is mostly optional, and the way to stop paying it
is not discipline in the moment — it is a boundary set before the moment
arrives. Sign the small contract, put the thought on paper, and let the
bell decide when the world gets you back.
