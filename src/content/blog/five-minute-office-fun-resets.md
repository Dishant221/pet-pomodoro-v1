Not every five-minute break is the same five minutes. Research on
micro-breaks at work has found that breaks combining real psychological
detachment with a small positive emotional lift outperform breaks that are
just idle time — the *content* of the break matters roughly as much as
whether you took one at all. Here's what that looks like in practice, not
just in the abstract.

## What the research says actually helps

A recent study on micro-breaks and concentration found that break activities
genuinely disengaging attention from work — rather than passively resting
while still half-monitoring it — produced measurably better subsequent
focus. Separately, workplace diary research on short breaks found that
detachment and a small deliberate mood lift work as two distinct,
complementary mechanisms, not one substituting for the other.

Translated into an actual five minutes between meetings, that means: pick
something specific, pick something that isn't work-adjacent, and let it run
its full length rather than checking the clock at minute two.

## Five things that fit in five minutes

- **Feed or stroke a companion** on your screen — thirty seconds, but it's a
  genuine full stop, and [the detachment mechanism behind why this actually
  helps](/office-hours-virtual-pet-breaks/) is the same one the research
  above is describing.
- **Step away from the desk entirely.** Even a short walk to another room
  changes the visual environment, which does more for detachment than
  staying in the chair that's associated with the work itself.
- **A two-minute unscramble or memory game** — genuinely trivial, genuinely
  not-work, which is precisely the point; it occupies attention just enough
  to crowd out rehearsing the next meeting.
- **Water, out loud, on purpose.** Small and physical beats scrolling,
  because scrolling is itself attention-demanding in a way that doesn't
  actually rest anything.
- **A short chat that isn't about a deliverable.** The coworker
  coffee-break conversation works for the same reason the pet does — it's
  bounded, social, and unrelated to the work you're about to return to.

| Break style | Detachment achieved | Effort to start |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scrolling a feed | Low — still passively attention-demanding | Low |
| Staring at the next meeting's agenda | None | N/A — not actually a break |
| A short, concrete, non-work ritual | High | Low |

## The point isn't the specific activity

None of the five above is magic on its own — what they share is being
specific enough to actually start and specific enough to actually end,
which unstructured "just relax for a bit" rarely manages. [Why breaks get
skipped in the first place](/office-hours-virtual-pet-breaks/) is mostly a
design problem, not a discipline problem — give the five minutes a shape,
and taking it stops requiring willpower at all.
