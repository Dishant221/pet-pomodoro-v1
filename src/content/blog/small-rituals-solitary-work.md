A ritual is a small, repeated act that marks an edge — the same mug filled
before the first task, the same chair angled the same way, the same button
pressed to begin. Done once, it is nothing. Done every working day, it starts
to behave like company, because it gives the day a shape that somebody seems
to be keeping. Even when the somebody is you.

## Offices were full of rituals nobody called rituals

Nobody who worked in an office thought of the kettle queue as ceremony. But it
was. Ten o'clock came, someone stood up, someone else followed, and for five
minutes the day had a hinge in it. The same went for the lift small talk, the
sandwich at the usual bench, the colleague who sighed at the same meeting
every week. None of it was work, and all of it was structure.

The useful thing about these rituals was that other people kept them for you.
You did not have to remember that it was time to stop; the kettle crowd
remembered on your behalf. The day's edges were maintained communally, like a
footpath that stays a footpath because everyone keeps walking it.

Then a lot of us moved home, and the footpath grassed over. The work came
along fine. The ceremony did not, and its absence is a large part of
[the isolation nobody schedules for](/blog/working-from-home-isolation/) —
not the missing meetings, but the missing kettle.

## A ritual is an edge, not a superstition

It is worth being clear about what a ritual actually does, because the word
carries a whiff of incense and nonsense. A working ritual is just an edge you
can feel. The commute was one: however grim the train, it drew a line with
*home* on one side and *work* on the other, and your head crossed the line
with your body.

A day without edges does not become free. It becomes liquid. Work seeps into
breakfast, breaks blur into scrolling, and the evening never quite announces
itself — which is why a
[work-from-home routine built around breaks](/blog/work-from-home-routine/)
matters more at a kitchen table than it ever did at a desk someone else owned.

Rituals redraw the lines. The mug says *starting*. The closed laptop, carried
to another room, says *finished*. These are signals sent from you to you, and
they work for the same reason a written note works better than a mental one:
once a thing exists outside your head, your head stops having to hold it.

## What a good start ritual looks like

The start is the edge most worth building, because starting is the moment
solitary work feels most solitary. Nobody is settling in around you. The
silence has to be broken by you alone.

A good start ritual is small, physical, always identical, and ends with the
work actually beginning. Fill the mug. Write the one task on paper. Press
start. The identical part matters most — the whole point is that you stop
deciding. A ritual you have to think about is just another task.

This is one place a companion earns its keep. In PetPomo, pressing start
sends the cat to sleep — every time, without variation. The ritual has a
second participant: you begin, it curls up, and the settling-in feeling that
an office room full of people used to provide is performed, in miniature, by
one small animal. It is a modest effect. It is also surprisingly hard to
give up once you have it.

## The bell is a ritual too — and this one has a witness

Ends need marking as much as beginnings, and this is where solitary rituals
usually fall apart. Alone, there is nothing to stop for. The break you meant
to take at eleven dissolves, because breaks kept only by willpower are kept
by nobody.

A ritual with a witness holds better. When the bell goes and the cat wakes
up wanting feeding, the break has content and a claimant. You are not
vaguely *pausing* — you are doing the small ceremony of looking after
something, and the something notices whether you did.
[Why looking after a creature helps](/blog/why-caring-for-something-helps/)
when you spend your days alone is a bigger subject, but the short version
is: a kept appointment feels different from a lapsed intention, even when
the other party weighs nothing and lives in a browser tab.

Many people find the same effect with entirely analogue witnesses — a real
cat, a plant watered at noon, a neighbour waved at daily. The mechanism does
not care how the witness is implemented. It cares that the ritual is shared.

## Repetition is comfort, not a rut

There is a suspicion worth answering: that doing the same small things every
day is how a life goes stale. But a rut and a rhythm are not the same thing.
A rut is repetition you resent, in service of nothing. A rhythm is repetition
you chose, holding a shape you wanted.

The sameness is what makes it work. A ritual that varies demands attention;
a ritual that never varies releases it. The mug, the note, the sleeping cat —
because they are always the same, they cost nothing to perform, and the
attention they save is spent on the work and on noticing that the day, for
all its solitude, has a keeper.

Start smaller than feels worthwhile. One edge — a real beginning or a real
end, marked the same way every day for a week. If the day feels slightly more
inhabited by Friday, add a second. If you want a witness who always turns up,
[the timer with the cat](/) is one; the kettle and the notebook were doing
the job long before it existed.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
loneliness or the weight of working alone is pressing on you, a doctor or a
specialist is the right person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.
