An ADHD-friendly study session starts before the timer does: one task chosen
and named, one clear surface, and somewhere to put the distractions you already
know are coming. What follows is a single session walked through from first
minute to last — not a list of tips, because tips have a way of becoming
another list you never open, but the actual shape of half an hour that has a
reasonable chance of happening.

## Before you sit down: choose one thing and say it out loud

The session is decided in the thirty seconds before it begins. Not "revise
chemistry" — that is a subject, not a task, and a brain that struggles with
task initiation will bounce off it like a bird off a window. Something with an
edge: "redo the three equations I got wrong on Tuesday." If you cannot say
what finishing looks like, the task is still too big.

Then clear the launch pad. Not the whole desk, not the whole room — the
tidying urge that arrives the moment you decide to study is a decoy, and it
knows it. You need the one book, the one pen, and water within reach so that
"I'm thirsty" cannot become an expedition. Everything else can stay where it
is, judging you silently.

## The first minute is the whole battle

Starting is the hardest part of studying with ADHD, and it deserves its own
plan rather than its own shame. There is a longer argument about why in
[the piece on task initiation](/blog/adhd-task-initiation/), but the short
version is this: the wall between intending and beginning does not respond to
willpower, it responds to shrinking the commitment and adding a ritual.

The commitment is one session. Not the whole chapter, not "until it's done" —
one bounded block with a bell at the end. And the ritual is whatever marks the
threshold for you. Pressing the start button works because it is a physical
act with an immediate consequence: in PetPomo, the cat lies down and goes to
sleep, and the room goes quiet. Something changed because you started. That is
more than most first minutes offer.

Do not wait to feel ready. Readiness is a rumour. Start badly — reread
yesterday's notes, copy out a heading, do the easiest question first. The
first five minutes are allowed to be rubbish; their job is to exist.

## Minutes five to twenty-five: the notepad does the heavy lifting

Once you are in, the enemy changes shape. It stops being the wall and becomes
the swarm: remember to reply to Sam, is the washing done, what was that song,
I should check the exam dates. Each thought arrives wearing a costume that
says *urgent*.

Keep a scrap of paper beside you — actual paper, because unlocking a phone to
note something down is opening a door into a corridor of other doors. When a
thought lands, write it in four words and return to the work. You are not
suppressing the thought; you are giving it a seat and telling it to wait.
Written down, it usually stops shouting, because being heard was most of what
it wanted.

Some sessions you will glance at the pad afterwards and find nothing on it
mattered. That is not wasted paper. That is the sound of twenty distractions
that did not cost you twenty restarts.

## The break is part of the session, not a reward for it

When the bell rings, stop — even mid-sentence, even if it is going well.
Then take a break with actual content in it. Stand up. Look at something
further away than your desk. Eat the snack. If you use PetPomo, this is when
the cat wakes and wants feeding, which sounds like a gimmick until you notice
it has made you leave the chair, which was the point.

What a break is not: a different screen. Scrolling is not rest, it is
substitution, and it has no natural end, which is exactly the property a
five-minute break cannot afford. The break's job is to let you come back —
and coming back is easier when the break was genuinely different from the
work.

If you study alone and the silence itself is part of what makes sessions hard
to face, that is a real thing and not a character flaw — there is a separate
piece on [studying alone without feeling alone](/blog/studying-alone-without-feeling-alone/)
about making a room feel inhabited.

## When the session collapses

Some sessions collapse. You look up and you have been on your phone for
fifteen minutes, or you followed a footnote into a wiki spiral, or the task
turned out to be three tasks in a coat. This will happen and it does not need
a court hearing.

A collapsed session does not count, and that is all it is — not counted. Not
punished, not carried forward as debt, not proof of anything about you. Take
the break anyway (you clearly needed one), shrink the task further, and start
another session or don't. One honest session that happened beats three ideal
ones that didn't.

This matters double during long campaigns like revision, where the schedule
runs for weeks and one bad afternoon can feel like the whole plan failing —
there is more on pacing that in
[the exam prep piece](/blog/exam-prep-study-companion/). The plan did not
fail. An afternoon did, and afternoons are replaceable.

## Last minute: close the loop on purpose

End the final session deliberately rather than letting it dribble out. Note
where you stopped and what comes next — one line, "next: question 4" — so
tomorrow's first minute has somewhere to stand. Deal with the notepad list, or
bin it. Then stop being a student for the day, on purpose, rather than
drifting off with the books still open and a vague sense of unfinishedness
following you to dinner.

None of this requires an app; a kitchen timer and a torn envelope will do the
job. A [timer with a cat attached](/) adds a witness and a reason the start
button feels like a threshold, which for some people is the missing piece and
for others is noise. You know which you are after about a week.

*PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
focus problems are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the right
person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.*
