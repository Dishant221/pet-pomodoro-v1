The real focus blocks between meetings are usually one pomodoro long. The
classic gap — forty minutes between one call ending and the next beginning —
is too short to start anything grand and too long to surrender, and a single
bounded session fits it almost exactly. The skill is not finding more time.
It is treating the time you already have as usable.

## The forty-minute gap problem

Everyone with a meeting-heavy calendar knows the gap. The call ends at ten
past; the next one starts at ten to. What lives in between is a strange dead
zone: too short, you feel, to open the real work — by the time you have
found your place in it, you would have to leave again — and too long to
spend honourably on nothing. So it gets spent dishonourably on nothing:
email is checked, Slack is scrolled, a document is opened and looked at
rather than worked on. The gap closes, the next call begins, and the day's
verdict is already forming: *no time to actually work today*.

Multiply the gap by four or five and the arithmetic becomes uncomfortable.
The dead zones between meetings often add up to more than two hours — the
largest block of discretionary time in the day, dissolved into nothing by
the belief that it was too fragmented to count.

## Deep work doesn't fit; a session does

The belief is half right. The gap really is too short for the open-ended,
settle-in-and-lose-yourself mode people mean by deep work. That mode wants
an unbroken horizon, and the gap has a wall at both ends.

But a pomodoro was never open-ended. A bounded session doesn't need a
horizon; it needs edges, and the gap supplies both — one meeting just built
the start line and the next has already built the finish. Twenty-five
minutes on [one named task](/blog/one-task-per-session/) fits inside forty
with room for a breath on either side. It will not produce a masterpiece.
It will reliably produce twenty-five minutes of actual progress, which is
twenty-five minutes more than the scroll produces, four or five times a day.

The reframe matters: you are not failing to do deep work in the gap. You are
doing gap-shaped work in a gap.

## Defend the edges

Two habits decide whether the session happens.

The first is starting fast. The gap begins dying the moment the call ends —
two minutes of drift becomes ten with no perceptible transition. The
counter-move is to make starting mechanical: task named before the meeting
ends (written down, one line), timer started within a couple of minutes of
leaving the call. No inbox first, no "quick look" at anything. The inbox is
a corridor lined with doors, and you have twenty-five minutes.

The second is sizing the session to the gap, not the other way round. A
thirty-minute gap holds a twenty-minute session perfectly well — PetPomo's
session length is adjustable for exactly this, and the cat sleeps just as
soundly for twenty minutes as for twenty-five. A session that ends five
minutes before the next call is a success. A session that was still running
when the call started is how the whole practice gets abandoned.

Those few spare minutes at the end are not waste, either. They are the
break, miniaturised: stand up, look out of a window, feed the cat. Arriving
at the next meeting having rested for three minutes beats arriving having
worked until the final second, slightly breathless and still half inside
the task.

## The bell is a meeting-safe exit

Here is the quiet reason a timer works in the gap when willpower doesn't:
the fear of over-running is what stops most people descending into real work
between meetings. Without a timer, going deep is risky — you might surface
at five past, flustered and late — so attention hedges, staying shallow
enough to keep one eye on the clock. And clock-watching is just
[context switching](/blog/cost-of-context-switching/) in slow motion: every
glance at the time is a small unload and reload of the work.

The bell removes the hedge. Something reliable is standing guard, so
attention is free to go all the way down. You will be pulled out in time,
composed, with a note of where to resume. The bell isn't the interruption —
the meeting was always going to interrupt. The bell is what lets you commit
knowing the exit is handled.

There is a small pleasure, too, in walking into the next call having
actually finished something, with a woken cat and a coin to show for it
rather than forty minutes of residue.

## A day of gaps is still a day

None of this rescues a calendar that is genuinely wall-to-wall; some days
hold no gaps and pretending otherwise helps nobody. But most meeting-heavy
days hold three or four, and three or four finished sessions is — by the
[honest arithmetic of what a day actually contains](/blog/plan-your-day-in-pomodoros/)
— a respectable day's focused work, extracted from time that was previously
written off.

It helps to plan the gaps like the sessions they are: look at the calendar
in the morning, spot where they fall, and assign each one a task in advance,
the way you would any other block. The [office-adapted version of the
technique](/blog/pomodoro-at-work/) is mostly this kind of salvage — not
carving out mythical four-hour stretches, but refusing to let the real
forty-minute ones dissolve.

The gaps were always there. A [timer](/) doesn't create them. It just stops
them being spent on the peculiar, tiring non-rest of waiting for the next
meeting to start.
