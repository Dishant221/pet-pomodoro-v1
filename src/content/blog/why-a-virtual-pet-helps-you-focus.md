Most people who try a focus timer quit within a fortnight. Not because the
method is wrong — the [Pomodoro Technique](/what-is-the-pomodoro-technique/)
is about as simple as a productivity method gets — but because a timer is a
number that goes down, and abandoning a number costs nothing.

## Nothing is watching

Press stop on a plain timer and the world is unchanged. There is no cost, no
record, nobody who notices. Every habit-formation model wants a consequence
attached to the behaviour, and a countdown has none.

This is why streak counters caught on. A streak is a cheap way to manufacture
a cost: breaking it destroys something you built. It works, right up until you
miss a day — and then, because the thing you were protecting is already gone,
a lot of people stop entirely.

## Care is a stronger lever than score

A virtual pet borrows a much older motivation. Looking after something that
depends on you is a drive most people have without being trained into it, and
it has properties a streak does not:

- **It is present tense.** The pet is hungry now, not "you have broken a
  47-day record".
- **It is forgiving.** Neglect makes it sad; it does not delete your progress.
  You can always come back and feed it.
- **It is specific.** "Mochi is hungry" is a clearer instruction than "your
  productivity score dropped."

Tamagotchi understood this in 1996, and every successful focus app since Forest
has been rediscovering it.

## The design decision that matters

The interesting question is *when* the pet reacts.

Get it wrong and the pet becomes the distraction — an animal doing something
adorable in the corner of the screen is precisely the thing a focus tool must
not be. The fix is to make the pet **least interesting while you are working**.

In PetPomo, starting a session sends the cat to sleep. It curls up, it stops
asking for anything, and the screen goes quiet. Everything social — begging,
purring, playing, bringing you a present — happens during breaks, when
attention is the thing you are supposed to be spending.

So the pet is a reason to *start* and a reason to *not abandon*, and it stays
out of the way in between. That ordering is the whole trick.

## Where the guilt line is

There is a real ethical edge here. It is easy to build something that makes
people feel genuinely bad for not using it, and plenty of apps do — a plant
that dies, a pet that runs away, a streak that shames you.

We deliberately stopped short. The cat gets sad. It does not die, it cannot be
lost, and nothing is ever destroyed. Coming back after two weeks away should
feel like a reunion, not an inspection.

A tool that makes you dread opening it is not a productivity tool.

## What the research offers

Nobody has run a trial on pixel cats and pomodoros specifically. What exists
is solid research on the ingredients — care, presence, and how long habits
actually take:

| Finding | Key numbers | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interacting with animals lowers stress hormones and raises bonding ones | Review of 69 studies: reduced cortisol, blood pressure and heart rate; oxytocin proposed as the shared mechanism | [Beetz et al., *Frontiers in Psychology* (2012)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00234) |
| An animal's presence beats a friend's for staying calm under pressure | Stress reactivity during hard tasks was lowest with a pet present — lower than with a friend or spouse | [Allen et al., *Psychosomatic Medicine* (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1097/00006842-200209000-00005) |
| People treat even robotic pets as social companions | Children directed real social behaviour at a robot dog, not just at live dogs | [Melson et al., *Journal of Social Issues* (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4560.2009.01613.x) |
| Habits need far longer than a fortnight to stick | Median 66 days to automaticity (range 18–254) — most apps are abandoned long before the habit had a chance | [Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) |

The honest reading: the calming effects are best documented for real animals,
and a virtual pet inherits them only partially. The Melson finding is the
interesting one — the social response survives the animal being artificial —
and the Lally numbers explain why a reason to come back matters more than any
feature.

## Does it actually work?

For some people, plainly yes; for others a bare timer is better, and there is
no shame in that. If deadlines and a clean desk are enough for you, adding a
cat is noise.

But if you have started a focus timer forty times and finished it four, the
problem is not that you need a stricter method. It is that nothing yet has
made finishing feel like it mattered.

[Try a session](/) and see whether it changes anything. It takes 25 minutes to
find out.
