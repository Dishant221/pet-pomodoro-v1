A streak counter is a bet: the number will keep you coming back. For a
while it does. Then, on the day it doesn't — a sick day, a bad week, one
missed evening — a lot of people don't quietly restart at zero. They stop
opening the app at all. That's not a willpower failure. It's a documented
psychological pattern with a name, and it's worth understanding before
building your own consistency around a number that can hit zero.

## The abstinence violation effect

Addiction researchers Alan Marlatt and Judith Gordon named this pattern the
abstinence violation effect: when someone holding an all-or-nothing view of
their own progress has a single lapse, they tend to interpret it not as a
minor setback but as proof the whole effort has failed — total, not partial.
The psychological damage isn't done by the lapse itself. It's done by what
the person tells themselves the lapse *means*. Meta-analyses in that
literature have found relapse rates as high as roughly two in three after
a single lapse, concentrated heavily among people who held the strictest
all-or-nothing framing going in.

A habit streak is a small, everyday version of the exact same trap. A
counter that goes from 47 to 0 overnight isn't just tracking a miss — by its
design, it's actively asserting that the previous 47 days no longer count
for anything.

## What that costs, and what it doesn't have to

The frustrating part is that the underlying habit research doesn't actually
support the harsh framing. Studies on real habit formation have found that
missing a single day has no measurable effect on how strongly a habit
eventually forms — what actually derails a habit is missing *many* days in a
row, which is usually a *consequence* of the all-or-nothing thinking a
broken streak triggers, not an independent problem on its own. The number
resetting to zero isn't reporting a fact about your habit. It's manufacturing
a crisis your actual behavior didn't create.

| What the streak counter implies | What the research actually shows |
| --- | --- |
| One missed day erases all prior progress | A single missed day has little measurable effect on habit strength |
| Consistency is binary — you either kept the streak or you didn't | Consistency is closer to continuous — most days count, roughly, toward the average |
| The number is a neutral fact | The number's framing (reset to zero) actively shapes whether you continue |

## A mood instead of a counter

This is the specific design PetPomo uses instead: the cat's mood dips a
little when you've been away and recovers once you come back, rather than
a number publicly resetting. There's no streak to break, so there's nothing
for a single missed day to "ruin." [Rejection-sensitive dysphoria and
apps that judge you](/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-productivity-apps/)
covers the sharper end of why this matters for some people specifically —
but the abstinence violation effect above is general psychology, not an
ADHD-specific pattern, and it's worth designing around either way.

If a streak-based app has ever made you quietly stop using it after one bad
day, that wasn't a personal failure to keep a promise. It was a number doing
exactly what that kind of number is built to do.
