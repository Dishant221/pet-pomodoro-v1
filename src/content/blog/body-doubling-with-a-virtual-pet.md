Body doubling is getting work done in the presence of someone who is not
helping. They sit nearby, doing their own thing — reading, typing, folding
laundry — and somehow the task you could not start alone gets started. It
sounds like it shouldn't work, and it reliably does; enough people with ADHD
swear by it that whole routines, friendships and online communities have
been built on nothing more than being quietly in the same room.

## The help is that they don't help

The strangest thing about body doubling is what the other person contributes:
nothing. They don't check your work, don't keep you on task, often don't
even know what you're doing. If they started supervising, the effect would
curdle — supervision adds pressure, and pressure is rarely what a stalled
start needs.

What their presence supplies instead seems to be two quieter things.

The first is a witness. Work done in front of someone is slightly more real
than work done alone. Getting up to wander the kitchen mid-sentence feels
different with another person in the room — not because they would say
anything, but because *you* would notice yourself doing it. The watcher
doesn't need to watch; it is enough that they could.

The second is borrowed structure. A person working nearby radiates a low
signal — *this is a time and place where work is happening* — and it turns
out that signal can be picked up second-hand. Their session gives your
session a shape. If your own executive machinery is unreliable, sitting
inside someone else's running copy is a very cheap patch.

Neither mechanism requires conversation. Both are wrecked by it, in fact.
The whole arrangement runs on parallel silence, which is its own odd,
comfortable kind of company —
[presence that asks nothing of you](/presence-without-conversation/).

## The company doesn't have to be in the room

Once you notice what body doubling actually supplies — a witness and a
running rhythm, no interaction required — you can spot people sourcing it
from further and further away.

A friend on a video call, both of you muted, working. A library, where the
whole room is a body double. And at the far end, the genuinely strange and
genuinely popular practice of
[studying alongside strangers on video](/study-with-me-quiet-company/) —
hours of footage of someone silently writing at a desk, watched by thousands
of people who are also silently writing at desks. Nobody in that arrangement
has ever met. It still works, for many people, because the mechanism never
needed acquaintance. It needed presence with a pulse of work in it.

Each step down this ladder trades away warmth and trades in convenience.
The friend knows you; the stream doesn't; but the stream is available at 6
a.m. and never cancels.

## Where a sleeping cat enters the argument

Keep descending the ladder and you arrive somewhere unexpected: a small
animated cat, asleep next to a countdown.

In [PetPomo](/), starting a focus session sends the cat to sleep beside the
timer, and it stays there — breathing, curled, minding its own business —
until the bell. As body doubles go it is the minimal case: it cannot talk,
cannot judge, cannot help. But look at what survives the reduction. There is
a presence at the edge of your vision. It arrived when the work started, so
it carries the session's shape. And abandoning the session means something
in the room *changes* — the nap you both agreed to is broken. A witness, a
rhythm, a mild cost to walking off. The mechanism is intact; only the
biography is missing.

There are honest advantages to company this small. It costs nothing
socially — no scheduling, no self-consciousness, no one to cancel on, no
performance of looking busy. Human body doubles are wonderful and scarce; a
cat that materialises at the press of a button is neither wonderful nor
scarce, and at four in the afternoon on a stuck day, available beats
wonderful. The case for why a creature on the screen holds a habit better
than a bare number is
[its own argument](/why-a-virtual-pet-helps-you-focus/), but the short
version is that people are wired to notice living things, even schematic
ones, in a way they are not wired to notice countdowns.

## What the smaller doubles can't do

Fairness requires the other column. A virtual pet will not text you "are we
still on for 9?" — the accountability of an arrangement with a human, the
kind you'd feel bad breaking, has no miniature equivalent. It won't
celebrate your promotion or notice you're having a rough week. And for some
people the witness effect simply requires a witness with eyes; a drawn cat
reads as decoration, and decoration doesn't hold anyone to anything.

That is fine. The ladder isn't a ranking with a correct answer; it is a
menu, and most people who use body doubling mix tiers — a co-working call
for the dreaded weekly admin, the library for deep reading, the cat for the
ordinary Tuesday afternoons that make up most of a life. The scarce, warm
company gets saved for where it counts, and the cheap, always-on kind
covers the rest.

If working alone consistently fails you and working alongside almost
anything works, that isn't a flaw to fix. It is information about how you
run, and it is far easier to supply company than to argue with.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
focus problems are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the right
person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.
