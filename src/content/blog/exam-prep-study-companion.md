A companion who keeps the schedule solves the hardest problem in exam prep,
which is not understanding the material but turning up to it, day after day,
for weeks. Revision is a long campaign, and campaigns are won on pace rather
than intensity — the steady sessions you actually do beat the heroic ones
you plan and dread.

## Pace beats intensity

Every student knows both versions of a revision day. There is the heroic
one: nine hours at the desk, highlighters uncapped, phone in a drawer, ending
in exhaustion and the sense of having earned tomorrow off. And there is the
unglamorous one: three or four honest sessions, breaks taken, finished by
late afternoon with something specific learned.

The heroic day feels better to plan and worse to live. Its real cost lands
the next morning, when the desk has become a place associated with
exhaustion and the day off quietly becomes two. Revision fails this way far
more often than it fails through idleness — not too few hours, but hours
spent in unsustainable bursts with craters between them.

The unglamorous day repeats. That is its entire virtue, and over the six or
eight weeks a serious exam demands, it is the only virtue that compounds.
Material met little and often has a way of feeling familiar by the end;
material met in one desperate fortnight has a way of feeling like a stranger
you once sat next to.

## The session is the unit of revision

Hours are the wrong measure for revision because an hour tells you nothing
about what happened inside it, and revision is unusually good at producing
hours in which nothing happened — reading that isn't absorbing, highlighting
that isn't thinking, notes copied from other notes.

A bounded session with a named question resists this. Not "revise
circulation" but "can I draw the circulatory system from memory and check
it?" Not "do past papers" but "attempt question three from the 2024 paper,
closed book". A session task should be something the session could actually
settle, so that the bell delivers a verdict: you can now do the thing, or
you know precisely what you still can't do. Both are progress. Vague hours
deliver neither.

What a single session looks like from the inside — choosing the task,
clearing the start, parking distractions on paper — is
[its own walkthrough](/blog/adhd-friendly-study-session/), and everything in
it applies double during exam season, when the stakes make attention
jumpier than usual.

## A schedule needs a face

Here is the odd, human part. A revision timetable on paper is easy to
betray, because paper doesn't notice. Nobody is watching a private
schedule, which is precisely why private schedules dissolve.

This is where a companion earns its place — not by teaching you anything,
but by being the schedule's face. In [PetPomo](/), the cat sleeps while you
focus and expects things at the bell: feeding, a stroke, a few minutes of
play before the next round. Keep the routine for a week and the routine
starts to feel *inhabited* — ten o'clock isn't just "session one" on a
timetable, it is when the cat settles down to sleep. Skipping stops being
a line through a grid and becomes standing something up.

That sounds like a trick, and it is one, but it is a trick in the right
direction. People keep appointments with others far more reliably than
appointments with themselves, and a creature that visibly expects the
routine converts a self-appointment into something slightly more binding.
Slightly is often enough. Revision doesn't need you to feel accountable to
the syllabus; it needs you at the desk at roughly the same times, most days,
for weeks — and company at the desk makes that
[considerably less bleak](/blog/studying-alone-without-feeling-alone/) than
doing it alone in silence.

## The panic week

Some weeks before the exam, pace's great enemy arrives: panic. The syllabus
suddenly looks enormous, the days left look few, and every instinct says the
same thing — drop the breaks, extend the sessions, revise like it's an
emergency.

It is worth being suspicious of this instinct, because it asks you to
abandon exactly the machinery that got you this far. Marathon sessions in
the panic week produce the same thing they produce in any other week —
diminishing attention dressed up as effort — only now with added dread. Most
people can feel the difference between the first session after a proper
break and the fourth hour without one; the panic week does not repeal it,
and [skipping breaks makes focus worse, not better](/blog/skipping-breaks-adhd/)
regardless of how loudly the calendar is shouting.

What the panic week may honestly justify is *more sessions* — an earlier
start, an extra round in the evening — with the breaks kept intact between
them. More blocks, not bigger ones. The cat still gets fed at the bell. If
anything, the small ritual matters more now: it is a few minutes in which
the exam does not exist, several times a day, on schedule.

## The exam ends; the routine doesn't have to

There is a small consolation waiting on the far side. A student who
revised on pace has, almost by accident, built the entire apparatus of
working steadily — sessions, breaks, honest tasks, a routine with a face —
and it transfers directly to whatever comes after the exam hall.

One caveat belongs here, plainly stated. PetPomo is a timer, not a
treatment. Nothing on this page is medical advice — if exam stress is
tipping into something heavier than the ordinary dread, a doctor or a
specialist is the right person to talk to, and a cat with a schedule is no
substitute.
