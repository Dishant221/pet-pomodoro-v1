Time blindness is the gap between knowing that time exists and feeling it
pass. With ADHD, the knowing is usually fine — it is the feeling that fails.
An hour registers as ten minutes, a deadline stays comfortably abstract until
it is suddenly on top of you, and "I'll leave in five minutes" turns out to
have been said forty minutes ago. The response that works for many people is
not trying harder to sense time. It is externalising it — turning time from a
private feeling into a public object you can look at.

## The clock in your head is an instrument, not a fact

Most people carry a rough internal timer. It is what lets them surface from a
task with a suspicion that lunch is near, or say "that meeting felt like an
hour" and be roughly right. Nobody's version is precise, but for most people
it is calibrated well enough to steer by.

With ADHD it often is not. Time tends to arrive in two settings — *now* and
*not now* — and everything filed under "not now" sits at the same distance,
whether it is due tomorrow or in June. A deadline three weeks away and a
deadline three months away generate the same amount of urgency, which is to
say none, right up until they cross into *now* and generate all of it at once.

This is not a character flaw, any more than short-sightedness is. It is an
instrument that reads wrong. And the sensible response to an instrument that
reads wrong is not to squint harder at it — it is to stop flying by it.

## Why "just check the clock" doesn't help

The standard advice is to look at the clock more often, and it fails for a
simple reason: a clock answers the wrong question. It tells you what time it
is, and time blindness was never about that. The question you need answered
is *how long have I been here, and how much is left* — duration, not
position. Reading 14:42 off the wall means nothing unless you remember when
you started, do the subtraction, and hold the result somewhere it won't
evaporate. That machinery is precisely the part that is unreliable.

There is a second problem. Checking the clock requires remembering that the
clock exists while you are inside a task — and being inside a task is exactly
when time-blind hours go missing. Advice that depends on the broken faculty
to repair the broken faculty is not really advice. It is the same instruction
as "feel time better", wearing a hat.

## Time has to become an object

What does help is representation you never have to compute. An hourglass is
the ancient version: duration made into sand, readable at a glance, no
arithmetic required. Half the sand gone means half the time gone, and you
know it the way you know a glass is half empty — by looking, not by working
it out.

A visible countdown is the modern equivalent. A number falling towards zero
is duration made literal; you don't estimate what's left, you read it. The
difference between *18:44 remaining* and a wall clock is the difference
between a fuel gauge and a map — one of them tells you the thing you
actually need to know, without asking you to derive it.

This is one instance of a much broader move: taking work your internal
machinery does badly and handing it to external machinery that does it
perfectly, every time, without being asked. That move deserves its own
discussion — [borrowing structure when your own is unreliable](/external-structure-adhd/)
— because it applies to far more than clocks.

## Fixed blocks give the countdown something to count

A countdown needs an end to count towards, which is why time blindness and
the [Pomodoro Technique](/what-is-the-pomodoro-technique/) fit each
other so well. The technique is, at heart, this whole argument in method
form: chop the day into fixed blocks, make each block's remaining time
visible, and mark the end with a sound you cannot miss.

The bell matters as much as the countdown. Edges you can *hear* don't need
to be noticed — they arrive on their own, from outside, and they arrive
whether or not you were paying attention. For someone whose sense of elapsed
time cuts out under load, an edge that announces itself is worth a dozen
edges you were supposed to check for.

Whether the technique as a whole suits ADHD is a fair question with an
honest, mixed answer — many people find it fits unusually well, some find
the standard intervals all wrong — and it is worth reading
[the full case](/pomodoro-technique-adhd/) before deciding.

## A state you can read is easier than a number

Numbers help; states are easier still. A number must be read and interpreted.
A state is simply *seen*.

This is the quiet reason a creature makes a good clock. In [PetPomo](/), the
countdown comes with a cat: press start and it curls up and goes to sleep;
when the session ends, it wakes. That gives you time as a state — cat asleep
means the block is still running, cat awake means it is over — legible from
across the room, in your peripheral vision, without parsing anything. It is
the hourglass principle again, wearing fur.

The same design keeps the clock from becoming the distraction. A sleeping
cat is deliberately boring. It changes state exactly when the information
changes, and does nothing in between — which is all you want from an
instrument you'll be sitting next to for half an hour.

## You don't fix the sense; you furnish the room

Nobody practises their way into a normal time-sense, and the attempt mostly
produces guilt. What you can do is build a room where time is lying about in
visible pieces: a countdown you can glance at, a bell that ends things
without your help, a creature whose posture tells you which side of the edge
you're on. None of this makes you feel time. It makes feeling time
unnecessary — which, for getting through an afternoon, turns out to be just
as good.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
focus problems are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the right
person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.
