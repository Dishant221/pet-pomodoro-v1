Loneliness erodes concentration because it is not a feeling that waits
politely until the work is done. It behaves like a background process —
always running, rarely visible, quietly spending the same attention you were
hoping to give the task in front of you. The work does not get harder; you
arrive at it with less.

## Part of you stays on watch

When connection feels uncertain, some portion of your mind keeps a lookout.
It checks whether the message has been replied to. It rereads the thing you
sent this morning and wonders whether it landed wrong. It notices, every so
often, exactly how quiet the flat is.

None of this is dramatic, and none of it is a character flaw. It is the sort
of low-grade vigilance you would expect from a social animal that is not sure
where it stands. But vigilance is attention. You cannot keep a lookout for
free.

So the lonely version of you sits down to the same spreadsheet as the
connected version, except with a second job already running. The spreadsheet
loses.

## Rumination wants the same chair as the work

Concentration happens in a small space. You can hold one genuinely difficult
thing in mind at a time, and everything else has to queue. Rumination — the
replaying of conversations, the drafting of messages you will never send, the
slow circling of *why has nobody called* — does not queue. It sits down in the
chair the work needed.

This is why lonely distraction has a particular flavour. It is not the fun
kind, the falling-down-an-interesting-rabbit-hole kind. It is the unread
group chat you keep opening — not because it is interesting, but because it
might contain evidence about where you stand. You check it, learn nothing,
feel slightly worse, and go back to the paragraph you have now read four
times.

A distraction you enjoy at least pays you something. This one only collects.

There is a small countermeasure worth borrowing from the pomodoro playbook:
when the worry surfaces mid-task, write it down instead of following it.
*Reply to Sam. Ask about the weekend.* A worry on paper loses some of its
volume, because half of what it was doing was shouting to avoid being
forgotten. You will not have solved anything — but you will have moved the
rumination out of the chair for twenty minutes, and twenty minutes is often
all a piece of work needed.

## The same task weighs more when nobody is there

Most people have noticed that a tedious job done alongside someone — even in
silence, even a stranger — feels lighter than the same job done alone. Part
of the effort of solitary work is that you must be the worker, the witness
and the timekeeper all at once. Nobody marks the edges of your day. Nobody
sees you start, so stopping costs nothing.

Add loneliness — not merely being alone, but feeling it — and the load grows
again, because now the lookout and the rumination are running alongside
everything else. Anyone who has [worked from home through a long quiet
stretch](/working-from-home-isolation/) will recognise the arithmetic:
the tasks were no harder than they were in the office, but somehow the days
cost more.

Naming this matters, because the usual conclusion people reach is *I have got
lazy* or *my discipline has gone*. Neither is the likely story. Attention is
going somewhere; it is simply not going into the work.

## Small, structural company is one lever — not the lever

The honest first answer to loneliness is other people: a call you actually
make, a standing arrangement, working an afternoon a week somewhere with
other humans in it. Nothing below replaces any of that.

But smaller, structural things move the needle too, and they are cheap enough
to try. A room that feels inhabited is easier to work in than one that feels
abandoned, and rooms can be made to feel inhabited by modest means — sound,
light, routine, [presence that asks nothing of
you](/presence-without-conversation/).

This is the mechanism PetPomo leans on. A cat that sleeps beside your timer
while you focus, and wants feeding and stroking when the break arrives, does
not cure loneliness in any deep sense — it would be dishonest to claim it
did. What it does is smaller and still real: it gives the room a second
occupant with its own schedule, so that working stops being something you do
entirely unobserved. Students seem to discover this instinctively, which is
much of why [studying alone goes better when the room feels lived
in](/studying-alone-without-feeling-alone/).

Be clear-eyed about the scale. This is a lever the size of a lamp, not the
size of a friendship. It is worth pulling because it is nearly free, not
because it is sufficient.

## Being kind about the bad days

If loneliness is taxing your attention, the worst response is to add
self-blame on top, because self-blame is just rumination with better
manners. The kinder and more useful response is structural: shorter sessions,
so that starting stays cheap. Defined breaks, so the day has edges someone
would once have marked for you. Something small and pleasant waiting at the
bell.

That thinking is most of why PetPomo exists at all, and why the cat gets sad
but never leaves — the longer version is on [the about page](/about/). A tool
for lonely hours has no business making them lonelier.

None of it makes loneliness vanish. It makes the working part of the day
slightly more possible, and on some weeks that is the difference that
matters. It also frees a little of the energy that self-blame was burning —
energy better spent on the phone call, the walk, the small real contact that
actually reaches the underlying thing.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
loneliness or low mood are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the
right person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.
