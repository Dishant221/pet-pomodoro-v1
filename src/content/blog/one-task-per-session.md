One task per session is the rule that makes the Pomodoro Technique work —
not the tomato, not the twenty-five minutes, but the discipline of naming a
single piece of work before you press start and letting the timer belong to
that and nothing else. Most pomodoros that fail were carrying three tasks
when they left the ground.

## A session with three tasks is a to-do list with a countdown

The failure looks innocent. You sit down and think: I'll use this session to
answer those emails, fix that bug and make a start on the slides. The bell
rings twenty-five minutes later and all three have been touched, none has
moved, and the session felt busy the whole way through. The timer can't
protect you from this, because the timer can't see inside the block. It
counts minutes, not intentions.

What went wrong is that the session had no owner. Three tasks in one block
means your attention spends the whole interval negotiating which of them
deserves the next minute, and negotiation is expensive — it is the same
switching cost you pay when a notification pulls you away, only self-inflicted
and continuous.

The [original method](/what-is-the-pomodoro-technique/) is blunt about
this: pick one task, not a list. It is the first instruction, and it is the
one that quietly gets dropped because it feels wasteful. Surely a whole
block for one small thing is inefficient? It isn't. A block that finishes
something is worth three that touch everything.

## Say it before you start

The rule only bites if the task is named *before* the timer starts. Named
means written or said, not vaguely held in mind — "sort out the report" is a
mood, "draft the summary section" is a task. One line on a sticky note next
to the screen is enough. When your attention drifts mid-session, the note is
the thing you come back to, and its specificity is what makes coming back
easy.

Naming also does something less obvious: it makes starting cheaper. A large
part of the [wall between intending and beginning](/adhd-task-initiation/)
is not knowing what the first move actually is. A session with a name has a
first move built in. You are not starting "the project"; you are starting
one sentence of it.

There is a small ritual quality to this, and rituals help. Write the line,
press the button, begin. In PetPomo the button also sends the cat to sleep,
which turns out to be a surprisingly firm way of marking that the session
has started — something else in the room has committed to the next
twenty-five minutes, so you may as well too.

## What "one task" means when the work is enormous

The objection arrives quickly: my work doesn't come in twenty-five-minute
pieces. A dissertation, a codebase migration, a business plan — these are
months, not sessions.

The answer is to slice by question, not by hours. "Work on chapter two" is
not a task, because you cannot tell when it is done. "Work out what chapter
two is actually claiming" is a task. So is "list the three worst risks in
the migration" or "find out why the test fails on Tuesdays". A good session
task is a question the session could plausibly answer, and you know you have
sliced correctly when you could tell someone else what finished looks like.

Big work sliced this way stops being a wall and becomes a queue. You never
face the dissertation; you only ever face the next question, and the next
question fits in a block.

## The rule is also a permission

One task per session sounds like a restriction, but most people experience
it as relief. For twenty-five minutes, everything else is officially not
your problem. The unanswered emails are not neglected; they are scheduled
for a different block. The other project is not forgotten; it is simply not
this session's business.

Without the rule, working on anything means low-level guilt about everything
else — a background scan of all the things you are not doing that runs
alongside the thing you are. The named session switches the scan off. It is
the difference between working on one thing and working on one thing *while
defending the choice*.

## The bell closes the loop

A single-task session has an honest ending in a way a multi-task session
never does. When the bell rings, the question either got answered or it
didn't, and if it didn't you know exactly where to pick it up — which makes
the next session cheaper to start too. [Closing loops](/finishing-what-you-start/)
is most of what a bounded session is for, and you can only close a loop you
can name.

It also makes the small celebration honest. In PetPomo the cat wakes at the
bell and a coin lands, and the reward means something precisely because the
session was one thing, attempted properly. A coin for twenty-five minutes of
diffuse busyness would just be noise.

## Sessions you can count are sessions you can plan

There is a final payoff. Once a session reliably means "one named task,
attempted for one interval", sessions become a unit you can
[plan a day with](/plan-your-day-in-pomodoros/). Hours don't work for
planning because an hour of blended attention is not comparable to an hour
of focused attention. Named sessions are comparable, and a plan built from
comparable units is a plan you can actually review.

None of this needs software. A kitchen timer and a sticky note enforce the
rule perfectly well, and [a cat that naps while you work](/) is an
accessory, not a requirement. The rule is the thing: one block, one name,
and the honest little verdict of the bell.
