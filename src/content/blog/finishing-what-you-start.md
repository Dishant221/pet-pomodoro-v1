You finish what you start by giving every piece of work an ending it can
actually reach. Most tasks do not fail in the middle; they trail off,
because nothing ever said *done* — and everything that trails off stays
open in your head, quietly costing you attention long after you stopped
working on it.

The fix is not more willpower at the end of tasks. It is putting the end
somewhere you can see it from the beginning.

## Open loops leak

An unfinished task does not wait politely where you left it. It follows
you. The half-written email drafts itself again in the shower; the
half-fixed bug turns up during dinner; the report you nearly finished
keeps a small process running in the back of your head, checking in at
intervals you did not choose. Anyone who has lain awake itemising
tomorrow knows the mechanism: the mind treats an open loop as a live
obligation and keeps refreshing it so it will not be forgotten.

One open loop is a background hum. A dozen is a head full of static —
and the static is why days with many started things and no finished
ones feel so much worse than the work involved can explain. You are not
tired from what you did. You are tired from carrying what you didn't
finish.

Closing a loop releases the whole cost at once. Which raises the real
question: what counts as closed?

## A bell is an honest finish line

"Done" is a slippery word for most knowledge work. The essay could
always be better; the code could always be cleaner; the inbox refills
as you empty it. Tasks like these have no natural finish line, which
means trailing off is their default ending — you do not decide to stop
so much as gradually find yourself elsewhere.

A timed session replaces the slippery question with a blunt one. You
name [one task](/one-task-per-session/), you start the timer, and
at the end there is a fact: the bell rang and you were still there, or
it didn't. No judgement about quality, no philosophy about doneness —
a finish line you either crossed or didn't, laid down *before* you
started, when you were still calm enough to draw it honestly.

This does two quiet things. It gives amorphous work a rhythm of real
endings — you may not finish the report today, but you can finish four
sessions on it, and four closed loops feel entirely different from six
hours of open-ended trailing. And it closes loops in both directions:
the bell that lets you stop matters as much as the one that says you
finished, because for some of us the failure mode is
[not being able to put a thing down at all](/hyperfocus-needs-a-bell/),
and an ending you did not choose is still an ending.

## Small completions deserve noticing

Here is the step almost everyone skips: when the bell rings, something
should happen. Not a reward exactly — an acknowledgement. A tick made
by hand, a stretch, a line through the task. The finishing has to land
somewhere, or your own head never quite files it as finished.

Most of us are terrible at this. We finish a thing and are already
mid-worry about the next one before the keyboard has cooled; the
completion happens but is never *felt*, which is why a productive day
can end with no sensation of having done anything. The loop closed and
nobody attended the closing.

This is why PetPomo makes a small ceremony of the bell. The cat wakes,
stretches, is visibly pleased; a coin arrives; the session lands in
[your record of finished sessions](/what-focus-stats-tell-you/)
where it stays counted. None of it is elaborate, and all of it exists
for one reason: the finish must be an *event*, not an absence of work.
Notice that the cat celebrates the bell, never the minutes before it —
the reward sits on the far side of the finish line, which is the only
place a reward teaches finishing rather than dabbling.

You can build the same ceremony with a notebook and a pencil. The tool
matters less than the habit of letting a completion be felt before you
reach for the next thing.

## Finished ugly beats abandoned perfect

The deepest reason things go unfinished is not laziness but standards.
The last tenth of any task is where it stops being the gleaming thing
you imagined and becomes the flawed thing you actually made, and
abandoning it *there* — at nearly-done — lets you keep the fantasy
version forever. An abandoned draft is still potentially brilliant. A
finished one is exactly as good as it is.

So the trailing-off is often a dodge, and it helps to call it one. The
ugly finished email gets a reply; the perfect unsent one does not. The
rough shipped feature helps someone today; the polished branch helps
nobody. Finished ugly can be improved later, by you or anyone —
abandoned perfect can only be carried, and you are already carrying
enough.

A session bell is useful here precisely because it is indifferent to
quality. It asks only: did you stay until the end? Practise crossing
that line often enough on small things and the crossing itself gets
easier on big ones. Finishing, it turns out, is less a virtue than a
habit with a muscle attached.

## A day with closed loops in it

None of this means every loop closes by nightfall — real work spans
weeks, and that is fine. The practice is smaller: end each thing *on
purpose*. A task is either finished, or parked with a written next
step, and a parked task with a note is a closed loop for tonight — the
head lets go of what the paper is holding.

Try it concretely: [one task, one session](/), stay until the bell,
and let the finish be noticed — by a cat, a tick, or just by you,
pausing for one breath before the next thing. It is a small ending.
But the days that feel finished are only ever made of those.
