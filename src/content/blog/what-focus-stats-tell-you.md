Your focus stats tell you two things worth knowing: how much you actually
focused, and when. They tell you almost nothing about whether the work was
any good, whether it mattered, or whether you are a diligent person — and the
trouble starts the moment you read them as if they did.

A mirror and a scoreboard look similar. Both are surfaces with numbers on
them. The difference is what you do next.

## A mirror shows you; a scoreboard judges you

You look in a mirror to find out what is true — hair sticking up, something
in your teeth — and then you act on it. You look at a scoreboard to find out
whether you are winning, and the question "am I winning?" applied to your own
attention is corrosive, because attention is not a competition and there is
no opponent except a version of yourself having a different week.

Read as a mirror, a stats page answers useful questions. When in the day do I
actually focus, as opposed to when do I believe I focus? Mornings, almost
always, say the numbers, while the calendar keeps optimistically booking deep
work at four in the afternoon. How many sessions does a real day hold, as
opposed to a hoped-for day? That answer alone will quietly fix your planning —
[counting a day in pomodoros](/plan-your-day-in-pomodoros/) only works
if the count comes from evidence rather than ambition.

Read as a scoreboard, the same page answers only one question — more or less
than last week? — and both answers are bad for you. More breeds streak
anxiety. Less breeds shame. Neither breeds better work.

## The numbers worth trusting

Two, mostly.

**Sessions finished.** Not started — finished. A finished session is a
concrete, binary event: you picked a task, sat with it until the bell, and
did not abandon ship. It cannot be inflated by leaving a timer running while
you make lunch, and it cannot be faked without noticing you are faking it.
It is the closest thing attention has to an honest unit.

**When you focus.** The shape of your week is more useful than its size. If
the record shows your finished sessions cluster before noon, that is not
trivia — it is instruction. Guard the mornings. Put the email and the errands
in the afternoon where the focus was never going to happen anyway.

PetPomo's [stats page](/stats/) is built around these two on purpose. It will
show you your sessions and your pattern; it does not rank you against anyone,
because there is nobody it would be honest to rank you against.

## The numbers that flatter you

Total hours logged is the classic vanity metric. It goes up when you leave a
timer running through a phone call, it rewards long unfocused sittings over
short sharp ones, and it feeds the oldest bad habit in knowledge work:
measuring attendance and calling it effort. An hour at the desk is not an
hour of work, and a number that cannot tell the difference is decoration.

Streaks are subtler. A streak feels like a measure of consistency, but it is
really a measure of unbroken consistency, which is a different and crueller
thing. It treats a week of good work followed by one sick day as worse than
seven mediocre days, which is plainly false, and it converts the record of
what you did into a threat about what you might lose. A number that punishes
you for having a life is not measuring your life accurately.

## The abandoned session that vanished is a feature

In PetPomo, a session you abandon is simply not counted. No red mark, no
"failed session" tally, no entry in the ledger. Some people find this odd —
surely the failures are data too?

They are, but they are data with teeth. A visible failure count changes
behaviour in exactly the wrong direction: people stop starting sessions they
are not certain of finishing, which means the timer only gets used on easy
days, which means it is useless on precisely the days it was for. An
uncounted abandonment keeps starting cheap. You can attempt a session on a
scattered, hopeless afternoon knowing the worst case is nothing — not a
stain, nothing.

The bell either rang or it didn't. What the record keeps is the ringing.

## How to read a bad week

There will be a bad week. The chart will be short and gappy, and the reading
you choose matters more than the week did.

The scoreboard reading says: falling behind, losing it, look what you were in
March. The mirror reading asks what the shape shows — and it usually shows
something specific and forgivable. The empty Thursday was the day of four
meetings. The thin patch is a cold, or a deadline that swallowed everything,
or a stretch where the real task was too vague to sit with, which is a
[naming problem, not a discipline problem](/one-task-per-session/).

Then it asks the only forward-looking question stats can answer: what is one
session I could finish tomorrow? Because the way back from a bad week is not
a heroic week — [it is one finished
session](/coming-back-after-a-bad-week/), counted, banked, and built on.
Your stats will record it without comment. The cat, it must be said, will be
pleased to see you regardless of what the chart looks like; [the
numbers](/) were never the part of the room that cared.

A stats page is worth checking about once a week, with the same spirit you'd
check a mirror on the way out the door — a quick look, a small correction,
and on with the day. Stare into either one for long enough and you stop
seeing anything true.
