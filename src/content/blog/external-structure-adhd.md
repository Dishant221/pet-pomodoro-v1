External structure means moving the machinery of self-management out of your
head and into the world: alarms instead of remembering, visible countdowns
instead of sensing time, another person's presence instead of summoned
discipline. For ADHD this is not a workaround to be graduated from — it is
the correct engineering response to an internal clock that is unreliable, in
the same way that glasses are the correct response to eyes that won't focus
on their own.

## The internal version keeps failing for a reason

The standard model of getting things done assumes a set of quiet internal
services running in the background: a clock that tells you how long you've
been at something, a scheduler that surfaces the right task at the right
moment, a supervisor that notices you've drifted and steers you back. Most
productivity advice is written by and for people whose services mostly run.

With ADHD, some of those services are patchy. Not absent — patchy, which is
worse in a way, because a service that fails intermittently invites the
belief that you could run it properly if you just tried harder. You cannot
try your way into a reliable time-sense any more than you can squint your way
into reading a menu across the room. The felt passage of time simply doesn't
report in — there is a whole piece on that particular gap,
[time blindness](/blog/adhd-time-blindness/), but the summary is that knowing
time exists and feeling it pass are different faculties, and effort does not
convert one into the other.

Once you accept that, the strategy flips. Stop renovating the internal
service. Replace it with an external one that cannot fail in the same way.

## Prosthetics, not crutches

The objection arrives on schedule: *isn't that a crutch?* Shouldn't you learn
to manage without the alarms and the timers and the borrowed bodies?

Notice that nobody asks this about glasses. Nobody suggests you wean yourself
off your prescription through effort and character. A crutch, in the sneering
sense, is a support for something that would heal stronger without it. A
prosthetic is a support for something that is not going to change, and using
one is not weakness — it is accuracy. You have assessed the situation
correctly and equipped yourself accordingly.

The dignity question matters because shame is expensive. People who feel
their scaffolding is embarrassing hide it, skip it, or dismantle it the first
week things go well — and then the second week arrives. The people who do
best with external structure are the ones who have stopped apologising for
it: the phone alarm labelled *leave now*, the timer in plain view of
colleagues, the friend on a video call for company. Glasses for eyes, clocks
for time-sense. Same category, same shrug.

## What good scaffolding actually looks like

Not all external structure earns its keep. The useful kind shares a few
properties.

**It is visible without being consulted.** A clock you must remember to check
has smuggled the unreliable service back into the loop. A countdown that sits
in your field of view — or a cat that is visibly asleep *because a session is
running* — reports time to you whether or not you thought to ask. In PetPomo
the sleeping cat is doing exactly this job: the state of the room shows the
state of the clock.

**It has edges that announce themselves.** The bell that starts a session and
the bell that ends it are both load-bearing. An external structure that only
starts things leaves you dependent on the patchy internal supervisor to stop
them, which is how lunch disappears.

**It survives your bad days.** Structure that requires enthusiasm to operate
is fair-weather rigging. A start button is one action; a morning plan of
counted sessions rather than vague hours is one honest list — there is a
practical walkthrough in [planning a day in pomodoros](/blog/plan-your-day-in-pomodoros/).
The test is whether the structure still functions on the day you are running
on fumes, because that is the day it was for.

**It does not punish you for lapses.** Scaffolding is there for when you
slip. Scaffolding that drops you for slipping has misunderstood its role.

## Other people are structure too

The most powerful external structure is not mechanical at all — it is
another body in the room. Someone working quietly nearby lends you their
steadiness in a way that is hard to explain and easy to feel: you stay in the
chair because staying is what is happening here. That effect is called body
doubling, and it has a spectrum that runs from a friend at the same table to
a stranger on a video stream to — at the cheap, always-available end — a
small animated creature that settles down beside your timer. The full
argument is in [body doubling, and why even a virtual pet counts](/blog/body-doubling-with-a-virtual-pet/),
but it belongs on this list because people often exhaust themselves with
mechanical fixes before trying the social one.

The common thread in all of it: the structure holds the shape so you can
spend your effort on the work rather than on holding the shape.

## Borrow freely, and stop auditing yourself

The final trap is metastructure: spending your energy evaluating whether you
should *need* all this. You do need it, or you don't; a fortnight of use will
tell you, and either answer is fine. A [free timer with a cat attached](/) is
one cheap way to run the experiment — visible countdown, audible edges, a
creature whose sleep makes the session's existence impossible to miss — but
the principle survives any particular tool. Borrow a clock. Borrow several.
The people who seem to run on effortless internal discipline are, on closer
inspection, usually standing in scaffolding of their own; theirs is just
painted to match the building.
