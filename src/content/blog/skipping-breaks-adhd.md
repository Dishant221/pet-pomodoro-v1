Skipping breaks does not buy you more focus; it borrows focus from the next
session at a poor rate of interest. With ADHD the temptation to skip is
stronger — stopping feels dangerous when starting was so expensive — and the
cost of skipping is higher, which is an unkind combination. The break is not
the pause between the work. It is half of the method, and it is the half
almost everyone throws away first.

## Why the break is the half everyone skips

The logic of skipping feels airtight from the inside. It took you forty
minutes of circling to start; now, miraculously, you are *in*. Stopping seems
like voluntarily returning to the start of the maze. So the bell rings and you
wave it away — *I'll just finish this bit* — and ride on.

Sometimes that instinct is answering a real question, because with ADHD the
relationship with stopping is genuinely complicated: when attention finally
locks on, it can lock on without brakes, and there is a separate piece on that
failure mode — [hyperfocus, when the problem is stopping](/hyperfocus-needs-a-bell/).
But most skipped breaks are not hyperfocus. They are ordinary bargaining, and
the bargain is worse than it looks, because it prices the break at zero. The
break is not zero. The break is where the next session's start comes from.

## Skipped breaks are borrowed, not saved

Attention behaves less like a tap and more like a well: draw steadily and it
refills; draw continuously and the water gets muddier without ever quite
stopping, so you don't notice the decline until you taste it.

Anyone who has worked three hours "straight through" knows the actual texture
of the third hour: rereading the same paragraph, twelve tabs, a strange
irritability, the task somehow both urgent and impossible to look at. You were
at the desk the whole time, so it counts as working — but the useful output
lived mostly in the first stretch, and the rest was a slow leak dressed as
diligence.

That is the loan coming due. The break you skipped at the first bell was not
deleted; it was deferred, with interest, and it collects itself as a foggy
final hour or as tomorrow's monumental struggle to start at all. People who
keep the breaks tend to notice something counterintuitive: the day holds
*more* real work, not less, because each session begins from something like
full.

The five minutes were never the price of the break. The price of skipping it
was the next session, and that is the more expensive item.

## A break needs content, not just absence

Here is the second trap, subtler than the first: taking the break but filling
it with nothing — or worse, with a smaller screen.

A break is not the absence of work. Sitting in the same chair, staring at the
same monitor, scrolling something engineered to be unputdownable, is not
rest; it is a third task wearing rest's clothing, and it has no natural end.
Five minutes of feed becomes twenty-five, and now the break has eaten the
session it was meant to serve.

A working break has *content* — small, physical, finite. Stand up. Fill the
kettle. Look out of an actual window. Stretch the specific parts of you that
have been holding a mouse. This is one place a pet timer earns its keep in a
very literal way: when a PetPomo session ends, the cat wakes up and wants
feeding or stroking or a moment's play, and tending to it is a bounded,
gently physical act with a clear finish. The point is not the cat. The point
is that the break has a verb in it, and the verb is not *scroll*.

The other thing a good break is not: an errand. "I'll use the break to reply
to those emails" converts rest into a second job. If working from home has
already blurred every edge of the day into every other edge, this matters
double — there is a companion piece on
[building a home routine around breaks](/work-from-home-routine/),
because at home the breaks are the only skeleton the day has.

## The sad cat is design honesty

PetPomo has a small rule that people occasionally write in about: skip your
breaks back to back and the cat gets sad. Not dramatic — no dying plants, no
shattered streaks, nothing lost — just a creature that visibly wishes you
would stop.

This is the app editorialising, and it is meant to be. A timer that lets you
chain sessions indefinitely with silent approval is flattering a behaviour
that quietly wrecks the method. Whether the pomodoro rhythm suits ADHD at all
is a fair question with an honest answer — covered in
[does the Pomodoro Technique work for ADHD](/pomodoro-technique-adhd/) —
but whatever interval you settle on, the structure only works with both of
its halves. A cat that droops after the second skipped break is the design
saying so, in the gentlest register available: someone here thinks you should
stand up, and it is prepared to look mournful about it.

You can ignore it. It forgives you. But it will not pretend.

## Practise stopping the way you practised starting

If starting was a skill you had to build deliberately — rituals, shrunken
commitments, external structure — stopping is the same kind of skill, and it
responds to the same treatment. Let the bell be a rule rather than a
suggestion — a [timer that rings at both ends](/) helps, though a kitchen
timer and a kettle will do. Put a verb in the break. Watch what the following session feels
like for a week, and let the evidence argue instead of this page.

The people who get the most out of focused work are not, in the end, the ones
who can push the longest. They are the ones who stop on time, come back on
time, and are still doing it in March.

*PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
focus problems are weighing on you, a doctor or a specialist is the right
person to talk to, and this page is no substitute.*
