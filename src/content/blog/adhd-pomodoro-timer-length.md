The number 25 gets treated like it came from a lab. It didn't — Francesco
Cirillo picked it because that's what his kitchen timer happened to be
shaped like, a tomato, sold in 25-minute-ish twists. There is no study saying
25 minutes is the correct duration for a human brain, ADHD or otherwise, and
treating it as gospel is why a lot of people quietly conclude the whole
technique "doesn't work for them" when the real mismatch is much smaller than
that.

## Where 25 actually comes from

It's worth saying plainly: the number is arbitrary. What isn't arbitrary is
the idea underneath it — bounded blocks with a hard, audible end — which is
the part that matters for [time blindness](/adhd-time-blindness/) and for
[getting started at all](/adhd-task-initiation/). The 25 is a starting
guess, not a diagnosis of how your attention actually behaves.

## What attention research actually says about duration

Sustained-attention research generally finds two things worth knowing here.
First, attention lapses can begin surprisingly early on a task — some studies
put the first lapses as early as 8 to 10 minutes in, with more consistent
performance decline setting in around the 20–25 minute mark for many people.
Second, and separately, the body runs on a much longer natural cycle:
sleep researcher Nathaniel Kleitman first documented an ultradian rhythm of
roughly 90–120 minutes of alertness, later validated in laboratory work by
Peretz Lavie, whose findings pointed to something closer to 75–125 minutes
of natural focus capacity before a real dip. Individual variation inside
that range runs 15–30 minutes wide.

Put those two findings next to each other and 25 minutes looks less like a
number and more like a compromise between "attention degrades early" and
"the body wants a much longer cycle" — which is exactly why no single
length is going to fit everyone.

| Finding | What it suggests for session length |
| --- | --- |
| Attention lapses can start at 8–10 minutes on some tasks | A shorter block may suit tasks that are naturally low-stimulation |
| Consistent decline often sets in near 20–25 minutes | The classic Pomodoro length sits right at this edge, not comfortably inside it |
| Natural ultradian focus cycles run ~75–125 minutes | Some people genuinely do better with fewer, longer blocks than four short ones |
| Individual variation is 15–30 minutes wide | "Standard" length is a starting guess, not a fit |

## Finding your actual number

The practical version of all this: treat 25 as a first guess, not a
commitment. If you consistently feel finished before the bell, try 15. If
the bell keeps interrupting something that had just started working, try 40
or 50. PetPomo's duration is adjustable in Settings for exactly this reason —
the countdown and the sleeping cat work the same at any length, because the
mechanism that helps isn't the specific number, it's having a visible,
external, bounded block at all.

There's a reasonable, mixed answer to whether the technique as a whole is
[actually backed by evidence](/is-pomodoro-technique-science-backed/) —
worth reading before deciding the method itself is wrong for you, rather
than just the number attached to it.

PetPomo is a timer, not a treatment. Nothing here is medical advice — if
attention or focus is a real, ongoing struggle, a doctor or specialist is the
right person to talk to.
