The five minutes between a focus block and the next meeting is technically
free time, and almost nobody treats it that way. It gets absorbed —
answering one more message, reloading a dashboard, staring at the next
item on the list — and the break that was supposed to be recovery quietly
turns into more work wearing a different hat.

## Breaks that don't happen aren't neutral

This isn't just a matter of willpower. Research on psychological
detachment — the ability to mentally switch off from work during a
break — has found that it's a genuine mechanism behind why some breaks
recover you and others don't touch your fatigue at all. A diary study of
short workday breaks found that breaks genuinely rich in detachment reduced
fatigue and improved subsequent engagement; a break spent still half-thinking
about the next task doesn't produce the same recovery, even if the same five
minutes passed on the clock either way.

The problem during office hours specifically is that almost nothing about
the environment nudges you toward the detached kind of break. A blank
five-minute gap between calendar events is an invitation to keep working
quietly, not a signal to actually stop.

## What a small, dumb ritual does that willpower doesn't

A virtual pet works here for an oddly literal reason: it gives the break a
*task*, and the task is specifically not work. Feed it, stroke it, watch it
react — thirty seconds of something that has nothing to do with the next
meeting, which is exactly the ingredient the detachment research points at.
It doesn't need to be a pet specifically. It needs to be something concrete
enough that "take a break" stops being a vague instruction and becomes an
actual, bounded action with a start and an end.

| Break type | What tends to happen |
| --- | --- |
| Unstructured 5-minute gap | Quietly absorbed back into work-adjacent tasks |
| "Just relax" instruction | Too vague to act on — nothing marks the start or end |
| A small concrete ritual (feed the pet, stretch, make tea) | A defined action with a clear beginning and end, easier to actually start and stop |

## Fitting this into a real workday

This works especially well stacked against [the cost of context
switching](/cost-of-context-switching/) — the same five minutes that
recovers you also gives your attention a clean edge to restart from, rather
than drifting from one half-finished thing into the next. A companion that
visibly reacts to the break — settles down, perks up, notices you're back —
turns "did I actually take a break" from a guess into something you can see.

None of this requires a long session. [Five minutes of the right kind of
office fun](/five-minute-office-fun-resets/) between meetings is enough to
matter, if the five minutes are actually spent detached rather than half
spent on the next thing already.
